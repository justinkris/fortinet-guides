Session 32 Chapter 10 — Hardware Acceleration

Offloading Configuration

Flow vs proxy mode, NP session offload settings, and how to preserve hardware acceleration where it matters.

Before You Begin · Real-World Scenario

One IPS Policy That Halved the Throughput

An ISP operating a mid-size regional network ran FortiGate at their core routing layer, handling transit and peering traffic for several thousand business customers. For most of its operational life, the FortiGate had been running clean flow-mode policies — simple permit/deny rules matched by address and port, all eligible for NP hardware offloading. CPU utilization ran around 15 percent. Throughput was consistently 18Gbps on a link rated for 20Gbps. The operations team was proud of their headroom. A new customer segment — a managed service provider running security services for small businesses — required IPS inspection as a contractual obligation. The network engineer responsible for onboarding the MSP built a new policy with IPS enabled and applied it to the MSP's address range. The policy went live at 3:00 PM on a Tuesday.

By 3:20 PM, the NOC had three tickets open from unrelated customers complaining about sluggish connections. By 3:35 PM, CPU utilization on the FortiGate had climbed from 15 percent to 85 percent. Overall throughput had dropped from 18Gbps to approximately 9Gbps. The engineer who had built the MSP policy was still logged into the GUI when the alerts started firing. The problem was scope: the policy matching the MSP's address range was written broadly — it matched source addresses in a /16 subnet that the MSP occupied, but the MSP's actual servers were in a much smaller /24. Traffic from hundreds of other business customers whose IP ranges fell within the broad /16 was hitting the IPS-enabled policy, triggering proxy mode processing, and pulling CPU resources away from everything else. Every byte of traffic hitting that policy was now going through the CPU, not the NP hardware. The collateral damage was instant and severe.

The rollback took five minutes once the root cause was identified — tighten the source address in the policy to the correct /24 and re-enable nTurbo to allow partial NP offloading of IPS-inspected sessions. The throughput recovered. But the incident generated a post-mortem that became a training case for the team's junior engineers: enabling IPS forces proxy-mode CPU processing for all traffic hitting that policy, and a broadly-scoped policy will capture far more traffic than intended. The right approach is tight policy scoping, flow-mode for bulk traffic that does not require deep inspection, and nTurbo where IPS is genuinely necessary. Getting these three variables right is what separates hardware-accelerated FortiGate from expensive x86 software.

Overview

The fundamental configuration choice that determines NP hardware utilization is the inspection mode applied to a firewall policy: flow mode or proxy mode. In flow mode, FortiGate inspects packets as they pass through, without buffering entire protocol streams. This is compatible with NP offloading — once FortiGate has made the initial policy and security profile decisions for a session, the NP hardware can take over packet forwarding for subsequent packets in that session. In proxy mode, FortiGate acts as an inline proxy: it terminates the connection on one side, buffers the complete protocol data, inspects it, and reconstructs the connection on the other side. This requires continuous CPU involvement and cannot be NP offloaded.

Session offloading is not binary at the session level — FortiGate makes the NP offload decision per session after the first packet. If the session's policy uses flow mode and no features that prevent offloading are enabled, the session is offloaded to NP hardware after the initial CPU processing. The CLI exposes the offload state of each session. nTurbo (also called IPS offload) is a partial acceleration mode: when IPS is enabled on a policy, nTurbo allows FortiGate to offload the NP-eligible portions of session processing while still sending packets to the IPS engine. It does not provide full NP offload, but it significantly reduces CPU load compared to pure proxy mode.

The `npu-vdom-based-offload` setting controls whether inter-VDOM traffic can be NP offloaded when traffic crosses VDOM boundaries via NPU VDOM links. This is a relevant configuration parameter in multi-VDOM deployments where traffic flows between VDOMs internally. Understanding when offloading is disabled, how to configure policies to maximize NP utilization, and how to verify offload state from the CLI are the practical skills this session develops.
