Session 07  ·  Chapter 03 — VLANs and VDOMs

Configure VDOMs

Virtual domains — isolating customers, departments, or functions on a single FortiGate appliance.

Pre-Reading Story

"When Two Customers Shared a Firewall — and Shared Everything Else"

A managed security service provider operated a high-end FortiGate appliance serving five enterprise customers out of their data center. To reduce hardware costs, all five customers were onboarded onto a single physical device — same routing table, same policy space, same ARP table. On any given weekday the device hummed along without incident: each customer's traffic flowed through its assigned interfaces, policies were namespaced by source zone, and no one complained. The architecture looked fine in Visio diagrams and passed every quarterly review. No one had formally documented that all five customers shared a single global routing table and a single forwarding plane.

Then Customer C's network administrator pushed a change to their internal BGP configuration — a new summary route that inadvertently overlapped with a subnet block assigned exclusively to Customer D. The FortiGate absorbed the new route into its unified routing table and began forwarding packets destined for Customer D's address space toward Customer C's infrastructure instead. Customer D's internal file server started logging connection attempts from IP addresses it had never seen before — all belonging to Customer C's workstation fleet. Security analysts at Customer D noticed the anomalous authentication failures within twenty minutes and escalated. By the time the MSSP's NOC identified the root cause, Customer D's legal team had already drafted a breach notification letter. Traffic from one paying customer had literally reached the infrastructure of another.

The post-incident review was blunt: the FortiGate had supported Virtual Domain (VDOM) isolation since the day it was racked. VDOMs would have given each customer a completely separate routing table, policy database, ARP cache, and administrative boundary — hardware isolation on a single physical device. A routing mistake inside Customer C's VDOM would have been contained to Customer C. The lesson was expensive but clear: on a FortiGate, multi-tenancy without VDOMs is not multi-tenancy at all. It is a single shared environment wearing a multi-tenant costume.

Overview

What Are VDOMs and Why Do They Exist?

A Virtual Domain (VDOM) is a logical partition within a single FortiGate that operates as an independent firewall instance. Each VDOM maintains its own routing table, firewall policy database, security profile set, administrative access controls, and network interface assignments. From a management perspective, an administrator scoped to one VDOM cannot see or affect the routing tables, policies, or sessions belonging to another VDOM on the same physical device. This hardware-assisted isolation is what makes VDOMs useful for multi-tenant deployments, internal segmentation between departments with conflicting security requirements, or separating management traffic from production traffic.

FortiGate supports two primary VDOM operation modes. In standard multi-VDOM mode, all VDOMs share the same hardware resources and the administrator can create and delete VDOMs freely. The "root" VDOM is the default VDOM created at factory reset — it typically serves as the management plane and often connects to the internet or upstream infrastructure. In split-task VDOM mode, the device dedicates one VDOM exclusively to management functions and another to all data-plane traffic, providing cleaner separation between the control plane and data plane at the cost of flexibility. Understanding which VDOM owns which interface, and which VDOM an administrator is scoped to, is critical for troubleshooting access issues in a VDOM-enabled deployment.

Resource allocation per VDOM is configurable for many system parameters — session table limits, concurrent connections, and bandwidth can all be bounded per VDOM to prevent one tenant from starving others. Administrators can be granted global privileges (access to all VDOMs and global settings) or scoped to a specific set of VDOMs, making it possible to delegate management of a customer's environment to that customer's own team without exposing any other tenant's configuration. Before your Socratic session, think about what "hardware isolation" means in practical terms versus what "logical separation using a shared OS" means — VDOMs land closer to the latter, but the question of how complete that isolation is under load is worth exploring.
