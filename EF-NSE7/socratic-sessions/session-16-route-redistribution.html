Session 16 Chapter 05 — Dynamic Routing Protocols

Route Redistribution

Safely sharing routes between OSPF, BGP, and static — and why uncontrolled redistribution creates routing loops.

Before You Begin · Real-World Scenario

The Redistribution Loop That Ate the Network

Irongate Colocation operated a mid-sized carrier-neutral data center in Denver. Their internal network used OSPF to distribute routes between customer cage aggregation switches and the core layer, while the facility also maintained eBGP sessions with three upstream transit providers for external connectivity. When a new operations engineer was tasked with improving route visibility — specifically, making certain customer-facing prefixes reachable from both the internal OSPF domain and the BGP-connected external transit links — he configured mutual redistribution: OSPF routes would be injected into BGP, and BGP routes would be injected back into OSPF. It seemed logical on paper. Both protocols would have full visibility. No route maps, no filters, no seed metric adjustments — just straight redistribution in both directions, because the engineer figured he could refine the policy later once he confirmed routes were flowing.

The cascade began within 90 seconds of the configuration being committed. OSPF started advertising BGP-learned external prefixes as type-5 external LSAs throughout the OSPF domain. BGP picked those up via redistribution, re-advertised them to the upstream transit providers. The transit providers saw their own routes reflected back with slightly modified metrics and, depending on their own BGP policies, some of them installed those reflected routes as preferred paths — causing traffic to loop through Irongate's network. Meanwhile, OSPF's LSDB began inflating as the redistribution engine fed it an ever-growing set of BGP prefixes. The three core OSPF routers — enterprise-grade platforms with 16GB of RAM — hit memory pressure within four minutes. Two of them crashed. The third crashed attempting to reconverge with the remaining network. Irongate's NOC received 340 alert notifications in under six minutes. The entire colocation facility was unreachable from the internet.

The post-mortem was stark and simple: bidirectional redistribution without filtering is one of the most reliably destructive configurations in network engineering. The investigation confirmed that the redistribution had been configured without a route map on either direction — OSPF was receiving every BGP route (900,000 at the time) as external LSAs, and BGP was receiving every OSPF internal route and re-advertising it externally. The correct design involved a route map on the OSPF-into-BGP redistribution that permitted only a specific prefix-list (the colocation's assigned address space), and no BGP-into-OSPF redistribution at all — instead, a single default route injected into OSPF was sufficient. The network returned to full operation 90 minutes after the crash, once the configuration was rolled back and the corrected route maps were applied. This session is about understanding exactly how and why that loop formed — and the FortiGate configuration objects that prevent it.

Overview

Route redistribution is the process of taking routes learned by one routing protocol and injecting them into another. It is necessary in environments where multiple routing protocols coexist — for example, a network that runs OSPF internally but connects to the internet via BGP, and needs the BGP-learned default route visible inside OSPF, or needs specific OSPF-internal prefixes advertised via BGP to upstream providers. On FortiGate, redistribution is configured within a routing protocol's settings by specifying which source protocols (connected, static, OSPF, BGP, RIP) should be imported and, optionally, a route map that filters or modifies the routes as they are imported.

The primary danger of redistribution is feedback loops. If Protocol A redistributes into Protocol B, and Protocol B redistributes back into Protocol A, the same routes can cycle between both protocols indefinitely — growing in number with each cycle and consuming routing table memory until devices crash. Even without a crash, redistributed routes can carry incorrect metrics, get installed over better paths, or create suboptimal forwarding. A redistributed route enters the receiving protocol with a default seed metric — in OSPF, externally redistributed routes default to type-2 external with a cost that must be explicitly set (the default is typically 20). Without specifying a meaningful metric, redistributed routes may be preferred over locally originated routes in unexpected ways.

Route maps are the standard control mechanism for redistribution on FortiGate. A route map applied during redistribution acts as a combined filter and attribute transformer: it can permit or deny specific prefixes (via match clauses referencing prefix-lists or access-lists) and can set metric values, metric types, or tags on permitted routes. Using a route map on every redistribution point — even if the initial map is permissive — is considered best practice because it provides an explicit place to add granular control later without requiring a protocol restart. Redistributing "connected" routes injects the directly attached subnets of a router's interfaces; redistributing "static" injects manually configured static routes. Both can be combined or filtered independently depending on the policy requirement.
