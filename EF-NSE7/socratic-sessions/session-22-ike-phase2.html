The New Subnet That Disappeared Into the Void

Northgate Manufacturing had operated a stable site-to-site VPN between their main production plant and corporate headquarters for just over two years. The tunnel was rock solid — daily production reports, ERP data synchronization, and IT management traffic all flowed reliably. In early spring, the plant's operations team deployed a new production automation system: a network of PLCs and SCADA servers on a dedicated subnet, 192.168.50.0/24, isolated from the existing plant network as a security measure. The IT team at the plant added the new subnet to the internal routing table, ensured the plant FortiGate could route to it, and assumed the VPN would handle the rest. The project went live on a Monday morning.

By Tuesday afternoon, the automation team was filing complaints. Their monitoring dashboard at headquarters could not reach any device on the new 192.168.50.0/24 network. The plant-side systems on the original 10.0.0.0/24 subnet worked perfectly. HQ-side engineers confirmed routing was correct. The plant FortiGate showed no firewall drops for traffic from 192.168.50.0/24. Packet captures at the plant firewall confirmed packets were leaving the correct interface. Nobody could explain where they were going. The HQ engineer spent several days checking access control lists, route filters, and NAT configurations. Everything looked correct. The automation team considered the VPN project a failure and began discussing a backup connectivity plan.

The resolution came when a network engineer with deep VPN experience reviewed the Phase 2 configuration. The IPsec Phase 2 SA had been built with explicit proxy IDs (traffic selectors) that specified the original subnets: local 10.0.0.0/24 matching remote 172.16.0.0/24. These traffic selectors define exactly which source and destination networks are encrypted and tunneled by that Phase 2 SA. Traffic from 192.168.50.0/24 did not match any proxy ID — the FortiGate correctly decided the traffic was not destined for the VPN tunnel and sent it out the default gateway instead, where it was dropped by the internet router. Adding 192.168.50.0/24 to the Phase 2 traffic selectors on both sides — a two-minute configuration change — immediately restored connectivity. The lesson was that Phase 2 proxy IDs are not automatically updated when new subnets are added to the network; they are an explicit statement of what traffic belongs inside the tunnel.

Overview

After IKE Phase 1 completes and the IKE SA is established, Phase 2 (also called quick mode) negotiates the IPsec SAs that actually protect data traffic. Phase 2 is faster than Phase 1 — it uses only three messages, all protected by the IKE SA keys negotiated in Phase 1 — and its purpose is to agree on the specific parameters for encrypting the actual data: the encryption algorithm, authentication algorithm, SA lifetime, and (optionally) the traffic selectors that define which source and destination IP ranges will be encrypted under this SA.

Traffic selectors, also called proxy IDs, are a critical Phase 2 concept. They define the scope of the IPsec SA: which local subnet and which remote subnet are covered by this particular SA. In a route-based VPN, proxy IDs are typically set to 0.0.0.0/0 on both sides (any traffic routed to the tunnel interface is protected), and routing controls which traffic actually enters the tunnel. In a policy-based VPN, proxy IDs are set explicitly and must match between peers. A mismatch in proxy IDs is one of the most common causes of Phase 2 failure, and new subnets must be explicitly added. Perfect Forward Secrecy (PFS) is an optional Phase 2 feature that performs an additional Diffie-Hellman exchange specifically for deriving Phase 2 keys, ensuring that Phase 2 keying material is independent of Phase 1 keys. This means compromising one SA does not compromise others. PFS adds computational overhead but significantly strengthens the overall IPsec security posture.

The IPsec SA lifetime can be measured in seconds (time-based), kilobytes (volume-based), or both — whichever limit is reached first triggers a re-key. Understanding the SA lifetime interaction between Phase 1 and Phase 2 is important for troubleshooting: Phase 1 and Phase 2 SAs expire independently and can re-key independently. In production environments, Phase 2 SAs typically have shorter lifetimes than Phase 1 SAs, meaning Phase 2 re-keys happen more frequently while the underlying IKE SA remains active and protects the re-key exchange.
