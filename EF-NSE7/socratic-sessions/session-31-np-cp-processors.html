The Firewall That Ran at 30% of Its Rating

A colocation provider in a tier-two data center had made a significant hardware investment. Their edge security was anchored by a FortiGate model rated at 100Gbps firewall throughput — one of Fortinet's flagship enterprise appliances, specifically chosen for its NP7 hardware acceleration. The purchasing team had done the math: peak projected traffic was around 40Gbps, so the 100Gbps model gave them comfortable headroom. Procurement signed off, the hardware arrived, it was racked and cabled, and the network team migrated policies from the previous device over a maintenance window. For the first few weeks, everything looked fine. Then they started measuring. Actual throughput: 28 to 30Gbps. A device rated for 100Gbps was handling 30% of its rated capacity. Management wanted to understand why they had spent $180,000 on a firewall that appeared to perform no better than the $40,000 unit it replaced.

The root cause took a week to find because the symptoms were confusing. CPU utilization was consistently high — 70 to 80 percent — which seemed to implicate an underpowered processor. But the NP7 hardware was idle. An experienced Fortinet engineer was brought in and asked a single question about the inspection mode configuration. The answer revealed everything: the network team had enabled proxy mode with IPS and full SSL inspection globally — applied to all policies, including the bulk file transfer and video streaming traffic that constituted the majority of the colo's bandwidth. Proxy mode completely bypasses the NP hardware. Every packet, regardless of content or size, was being buffered and processed by the general-purpose CPU. The NP7 processors in this $180,000 appliance were sitting essentially unused. The appliance was performing identically to a software-only firewall running on commodity x86 hardware.

The fix was architectural rather than a configuration toggle. The engineer worked through the policy base to identify traffic flows that genuinely required deep inspection — a fraction of the total — and left proxy mode with IPS enabled only on those specific policies. Bulk transfer traffic, video streams, and known-good CDN traffic were moved to flow mode policies that the NP hardware could accelerate. Within 48 hours of the change, measured throughput exceeded 85Gbps and CPU utilization dropped to under 20%. The NP processors were finally doing the work they had been purchased to do. The lesson was not that proxy mode or IPS is wrong — it is essential for high-risk traffic. The lesson was that understanding which processing path your traffic takes is a prerequisite for deploying hardware-accelerated FortiGate correctly.

Overview

FortiGate appliances contain specialized processors alongside the general-purpose CPU. The Network Processor (NP) handles packet forwarding, session lookup, and traffic shaping at line rate — it operates at Layer 2 and Layer 3 and can process packets without involving the main CPU at all. The Content Processor (CP) accelerates cryptographic operations: SSL/TLS handshakes, IPsec encryption and decryption, and signature matching for IPS and antivirus. Together, these custom ASICs allow FortiGate to achieve throughput numbers that would be impossible with general-purpose x86 processors alone.

The key concept is offloading: when a session meets the criteria for NP offloading, FortiGate hands the session's subsequent packets directly to the NP hardware. The main CPU handles the session setup — the initial policy lookup, the first-packet inspection — and then the NP takes over for the data transfer phase. This is why NP offloading is particularly valuable for long-lived, high-bandwidth sessions: the CPU pays the setup cost once, then the NP handles the bulk. Sessions involving proxy-mode inspection cannot be NP offloaded because proxy mode requires the CPU to buffer and reconstruct entire protocol streams, which the NP is not designed to do.

Different FortiGate models use different generations of NP and CP silicon — NP6, NP7, CP9 — with each generation expanding the set of features and traffic types that can be hardware accelerated. Understanding what each processor offloads, what prevents offloading, and how to verify whether a session is running through hardware or software is fundamental to both NSE7 exam questions on hardware acceleration and real-world FortiGate performance optimization.
